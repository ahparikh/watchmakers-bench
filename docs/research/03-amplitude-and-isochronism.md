## (a) Amplitude versus mainspring torque

- **Torque curve shape.** [V] A going-barrel mainspring gives strongest torque at full wind, a relatively flat middle range, and a steep fall near exhaustion (WatchTime; Wikipedia "Mainspring" torque-curve figures). The last few turns are "ineffective turns" whose force drops off sharply and are not counted on (vintagewatchstraps.com mainspring page, quoting Britten) [P].
- **How much it falls in 24 h.** [P, industry rule of thumb] "Torque arriving at the escape wheel should decline by no more than 15 percent the first day" (WatchTime/Brunner). A typical wristwatch barrel makes ~6 turns of wind for ~40–44 h, ~7.5 h per barrel turn (nickelsilver, WRT fusee thread) [P]. Winding and unwinding branches form a hysteresis loop (US 8,356,930 abstract, seen via search only) [P]. No measured N·mm-vs-hours dataset for a modern wristwatch calibre was found [X].
- **Energy balance per beat.** [V] In Hoyng's averaged equations the oscillator energy is λ²/2 and linear (viscous) damping removes energy at rate ∝ λ² (his Eq. 9: dλ/dt = −ελ + ∮m cosψ dψ). The escapement's energy input per period balances this at steady state, giving amplitude ∝ √(driving torque) for a constant-torque escapement (Eq. 19) and λ = (e/2πε)^½ for a single impulse of energy e (App. C). [P] SJX cites Conus's thesis that viscous pivot friction dominates damping of a lubricated sprung balance, which supports losses ∝ A². With Coulomb (dry) friction instead, losses ∝ A and A ∝ torque. Real watches sit between the two.
- **Typical amplitude values.** [V] Witschi: "In most modern mechanical movements, the amplitude typically ranges between 270° and 310° when fully wound"; lift angle "approximately 51°"; amplitude A = λ / (2 sin(πft)) from lift time t. [V] Horopedia: amplitude "decreases as this energy diminishes"; construction limits it to "maximum 280°–300° depending on the lift angle". [P] Service-bench norms: 275–280° full wind and >225° at 24 h (WRT "Mainspring strength"); BHI teaching material quoted as 270° typical / 310° max at full wind, minimum 200° at 24 h; an ETA 2892 "only drops 10% maximum at 24H"; a 1950s 30 mm Omega shows "perhaps a 40 degree drop" at 24 h (nickelsilver). Witschi's own worked example drops 288°→278° mean (vertical 284°→272°) between 0 h and 24 h.
- **Verdict on the brief's 280°→200°.** A healthy modern movement drops ~10–15% (280°→~240–250°) in 24 h. 280°→200° (−29%) is a tired-spring or dirty-train watch, which is fine for the "afternoon slump" puzzle but should not be the healthy default.

## (b) Why amplitude changes rate in a lever escapement, and the sign

- **Airy's theorem.** [V] Airy, Ex. 7–8: an impulse given exactly at the lowest point (balance at rest position, zero crossing) leaves the period unchanged; a force acting "before reaching the lowest point tends to diminish the time of oscillation, and that after it to increase the time". So: impulse before mid-swing → gain; after mid-swing → loss. He states explicitly that because the hairspring torque is proportional to angle, "the same equations which apply to the motion of a pendulum will apply also to the motion of a balance", and that Mudge's lever "possess[es] almost exactly the same properties as the dead-beat escapement", whose rate effect "may be made as small as we please" but "cannot, however, be made absolutely nothing".
- **Modern form.** [V] Hoyng Eq. 10: rate − 1 = −λ²/16 − (1/λ)∮ m sinψ dψ; the second term "is called the escapement error ... as computed already by Airy". For a single impulse of energy e at phase ψ₁ (Eq. 29–30): escapement error = −(e / 2πλ²)·tan ψ₁, which "either increases the rate (ψ₁ < 0, impulse before a zero), or reduces it (ψ₁ > 0, impulse after a zero)". Note the 1/λ² and tan ψ₁ = φ₁/√(λ²−φ₁²): a fixed impulse angle φ₁ matters more as amplitude λ shrinks.
- **Applied to the Swiss lever.** [V] US 9,323,223 (industry patent): the oscillator "is subjected to a resistant torque before the line of centres, and this causes a loss"; then "a drive torque firstly before the line of centres, which causes a gain, then after the line of centres, which causes a loss"; "the escapement thus produces a rate loss and this disturbance caused by the escapement is greater for small oscillation amplitudes". SJX (Dec 2025) gives the same phase-by-phase account: unlocking → loss; impulse before equilibrium → gain; impulse after equilibrium and the run to banking → loss; net loss. Stoimenov: the lever engages over about ±15° of a ±270° swing, so the balance is free for >88% of the period [V].
- **So which way does a real watch go?** The *escapement alone* makes a Swiss lever watch **lose more as amplitude falls** [V, three independent sources]. But bench experience often shows the opposite net sign: "the movement works faster in the end of power reserve, when the amplitude is smaller" (nevenbekriev), "if the rate is higher when the amplitude is lower ... check the hairspring is centred in the regulator pins" (Klassiker), "at very low amplitudes the rate can increase considerably" [P]. The gain comes from the hairspring/regulator-pin system (section c), which is deliberately set to over-compensate the escapement loss. The patent quantifies the compensation designers aim for: a hairspring-induced gain "of at least 2 s/d, or at least 4 s/d, or even at least 6 s/d, or even at least 8 s/d, at an amplitude of 150° with respect to an amplitude of 300°" [V]. Witschi's worked example (0 h vs 24 h) shows gains of +1.7 and +2.9 s/d where amplitude fell 8° and 23° and essentially no change where amplitude did not fall [V, but an illustrative dataset].
- **Magnitude.** [P] Escapement-only loss: order 2–8 s/d over a 150° amplitude drop, i.e. roughly 0.1–0.5 s/d per 10°, growing as amplitude falls. Net, pin-compensated watch: typically ±1 s/d per 10° over the 240–300° band (Witschi example, Ie values of a few s/d). Pathological pin contact: −53 → −19.3 s/d as amplitude fell 249° → 204° (Seiko Marvel case, ≈ +7 s/d per 10°) [P]. No source gives a single universal coefficient [X].

## (c) Other isochronism effects

- **Regulator pins (flat spring).** [P, consistent across watchmakers] With the spring centred and a correct gap, at very low amplitude the spring is free between the pins (full length → slow), at higher amplitude it touches the pins more (shorter effective length → faster), averaging out over the normal band (JohnR725). If the spring rests against one pin, the watch "runs fast at low amplitudes" and slows as amplitude lifts it off (JohnR725, nevenbekriev). Opening the gap "will slow the rate, more so for low amplitudes" (Klassiker). Witschi lists "clearance in the regulator pins" among the main isochronism factors [V]. US 7,648,265 (via search): "depending on the distance of the regulator pins, oscillations at small amplitudes can be speeded up or slowed down" [P].
- **Pinning point / stud, Breguet overcoil.** [V] A flat spring with a fixed outer pinning point cannot "breathe" concentrically; the eccentric development loads the pivots and disturbs isochronism (SJX overcoil article; Wikipedia citing Phillips 1861, who proved a spring whose centre of gravity stays on the balance axis is isochronous). The overcoil raises the last coil and curves it toward the pinning point so the body develops concentrically; Phillips' conditions fix the end-curve's centre of gravity. [P] With an overcoil, "the overcoil is calculated to be isochronous"; residual error is corrected by reshaping it: closer to the balance centre raises the rate at low amplitude, further out lowers it (nickelsilver). Overcoil watches also need a pin-free or wide-pin regulator, which is why they hold rate 0 h→24 h despite a 40° drop (nickelsilver, same post).
- **Hairspring quality / material.** [P] Steel springs: centrifugal effect on balance arms slows the rate at high amplitude while less pin contact raises it, partly compensating (nickelsilver). Modern Nivarox-class alloys remove most of this.
- **Knocking / rebanking at very high amplitude.** [V] Horopedia: beyond ~280–300° (lift-angle dependent) "a knock (rebate) phenomenon causes the oscillator to run away and results in an exaggerated advance". [P] Mechanism: the impulse pin strikes the outside of the fork horn (the lever is against its banking pin) and bounces back early, truncating the swing → shorter period → sudden large gain, with a "galloping" sound; timegrapher traces go chaotic and reported amplitude becomes meaningless. Thresholds quoted: ~320–330° at full wind for a modern 51–52° lift-angle calibre; Omega guidance quoted as <315° dial-up automatic, <325° manual; a Raketa case shows disorder "as soon as the amplitude reaches near 325 degrees"; overbanking (pin stuck behind the horn) needs ~350°. Causes: too strong a mainspring, fresh lubrication, dial-up position [P].

## (d) Analytic model used in the literature

[V] Airy (1826) / Hoyng (2015) averaged (KBM) equations, per oscillation: dλ/dt = −ελ + ∮ m cosψ dψ (amplitude); rate − 1 = −(1/λ)∮ m sinψ dψ (escapement error; the −λ²/16 circular-error term is a pendulum term and is absent for a Hooke's-law hairspring). Sign rule: impulse before the zero crossing → gain, after → loss; magnitude ∝ (impulse energy / oscillator energy) × tan(impulse phase). Single impulse of energy e at angle φ₁: Δrate = −(e/2πλ²)·φ₁/√(λ²−φ₁²). Everything below is this model plus an empirical hairspring term.
