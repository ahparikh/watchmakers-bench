21	## Invariants tested
22	
23	1. Half a tooth (12°) per beat, measured from any locked instant.
24	2. One tooth (24°) per full period.
25	3. Wheel never runs backward beyond 0.001°.
26	4. Wheel stationary while locked with the pin out of the notch.
27	5. Lever motion never shoves the wheel by more than 0.001°.
28	6. Balance reaches ±amplitude.
29	7. Fork stays within its bankings and rests on one whenever the pin is out of the notch.
30	8. Phase sequence per beat is exactly lock → unlock → impulse → drop → lock, on both pallets.
31	9. Pallets alternate beat by beat.
32	10. No tooth vertex is ever inside a stone (beyond the contact solver's tolerance).

## (a) Period formula and stiffness vs active length

- **[V] T = 2π·√(I/k).** Derived from τ = −kθ = Iα, giving simple harmonic motion with period

## (b) Poise error and the 220° rule

**What it is [P].** The Rolex patent defines static imbalance as "the product of the mass of the balance times the distance between its center of gravity and the axis of rotation ... typically measured in µg·cm or nN·m. With terrestrial gravity, 1 µg·cm corresponds to approximately 0.1 nN·m." It states, citing J.-J. Augsburger, *La mise d'équilibre des balanciers*, Proc. Swiss Chronometry Congress 1966 p. 324: the rate effect "is proportional to the imbalance itself", "inversely proportional to the inertia of the balance", "highly dependent on the amplitude of oscillation ... It is even cancelled out completely for an amplitude close to 220°", "varies as the sine of the azimuth angle between the axis of the balance ... and the vertical" (so zero when dial up/down) and "varies with the angle between the direction of the imbalance and the vertical", with two opposite vertical positions where it vanishes and two, 90° away, where it is maximal. A second zero exists near 400° (patent, description).

**Why 220° [P, derivation checked here].** Put the heavy spot at angle φ from the downward vertical when the balance is at rest (φ = 0: heavy spot at the bottom). Gravity adds the torque −g·u·cos φ·sin θ to the hairspring's −k·θ (u = mass × eccentricity; the sin φ part only shifts the rest position and is second order). Harmonic balance of sin(Θ cos ωt) gives a fundamental 2J1(Θ)cos ωt, so

    ω² = ω0² · [1 + (g·u·cos φ / (I·ω0²)) · 2·J1(Θ)/Θ]
    rate [s/d] ≈ 86400 · (g · u · cos φ / (I · ω0²)) · J1(Θ)/Θ

with I the balance inertia, ω0 = 2πf, Θ the amplitude in radians (zero to turning point, the timegrapher convention). J1's first zero is 3.8317 rad = **219.54°**, which is the 220° rule. The patent's claim 9 is exactly the least-squares inverse of this expression: `3H(θ) − 9H(θ) = 2·86400·g·bx /(I·(2πf)²) · J1(θ)/θ`, with x the 9H direction, so the model and the primary source agree on form and on the (2πf)² factor.

**Sign [P via patent axes, S via practitioners].** In the patent, bx > 0 (heavy spot toward the 9H index) makes 3H(θ) − 9H(θ) positive for Θ < 220°; in position 3H the 9H index is at the bottom. Hence: **heavy spot at the bottom at rest, amplitude below 220° → the watch gains; above 220° → it loses; heavy spot at the top is the mirror image.** Practitioners state the same: "influence is zero at about 220 degrees, and it changes from fast to slow under and over 220 deg", and the Greiner dynamic-poising procedure (Mark, watchrepairtalk, 19 Dec 2014) tests at 150–180° and takes the fastest vertical position as the one with the heavy spot down. Physical picture (forum, [S]): at low amplitude the heavy spot stays near the bottom and gravity stiffens the restoring torque like a pendulum; above 220° it spends enough of each swing past the horizontal that the net effect reverses.

**Dynamic poising at one amplitude is unreliable [P].** The patent, quoting Vermot et al. *Traité de construction horlogère* (PPUR 2011, pp. 190–200), warns that single-amplitude "rate to positions" poising "lacks precision" because other position errors (hairspring, escapement) are mixed in; Rolex's method fits the J1 curve across amplitudes either side of 220°. A side note: WO2012007460 recommends correcting at 137° or 316.5° (the zeros of J0) so the material removed does not itself unbalance; that is a manufacturing detail, not a rate rule **[U, not pursued]**.

163	## The chain of power, in one paragraph
164	
165	A coiled **mainspring** stores the energy you put in by winding. A **gear train** carries that
166	energy to the **escape wheel**, stepping the speed up and the force down on the way. The
167	**pallet fork** lets the escape wheel advance one tooth at a time, and each release is one
168	tick. The **balance wheel** and its **hairspring** decide *how often* the fork lets a tooth go,
169	so they alone set the rate. Everything else (hands, date, crown) is bookkeeping.

232	## (a) Amplitude versus mainspring torque
233	
234	- **Torque curve shape.** [V] A going-barrel mainspring gives strongest torque at full wind, a relatively flat middle range, and a steep fall near exhaustion (WatchTime; Wikipedia "Mainspring" torque-curve figures). The last few turns are "ineffective turns" whose force drops off sharply and are not counted on (vintagewatchstraps.com mainspring page, quoting Britten) [P].
235	- **How much it falls in 24 h.** [P, industry rule of thumb] "Torque arriving at the escape wheel should decline by no more than 15 percent the first day" (WatchTime/Brunner). A typical wristwatch barrel makes ~6 turns of wind for ~40–44 h, ~7.5 h per barrel turn (nickelsilver, WRT fusee thread) [P]. Winding and unwinding branches form a hysteresis loop (US 8,356,930 abstract, seen via search only) [P]. No measured N·mm-vs-hours dataset for a modern wristwatch calibre was found [X].

265	## Oscillator
266	
267	- **balance** — the oscillating wheel. Its angle from rest is `balanceAngle` (degrees). *(spike)*
268	- **hairspring** — the spiral spring returning the balance to rest. Its stiffness sets the period together with balance inertia.
269	- **amplitude** — peak balance angle from rest, degrees. Healthy at full wind: roughly 250–300° dial up. *(spike, as `balanceAmplitude`)*
270	- **beat** — one half-swing of the balance, from one extreme to the other. One tick. *(spike)*
271	- **beat rate** — beats per hour (bph). 18,000 bph = 5 beats/s = 0.4 s period. *(spike)*
272	- **period** — time for a full swing (two beats). *(spike)*
273	- **regulator index** — the lever that slides the curb pins along the hairspring, changing its active length and so the rate. Marked F/S or +/−.
274	- **timing screws** — screws on the balance rim; turning them in or out changes balance inertia and so the rate.
275	- **stud** — the fixed outer anchor of the hairspring. Rotating the stud carrier changes where the balance rests relative to the fork: the **beat adjustment**.
276	- **poise** — the balance's mass balance about its axis. A **poise error** (heavy spot) changes rate in vertical positions.
277	- **isochronism** — the property of keeping the same period at any amplitude. Its absence is **isochronism error**.

279	## Escapement
280	
281	- **escape wheel** — the toothed wheel the gear train drives; released one half-tooth per beat. `wheelAngle` is degrees of rotation in the direction of tooth tr
282	- **tooth pitch** — 360° / tooth count. 24° for 15 teeth. *(spike)*
283	- **pallet fork** (lever) — the pivoted piece carrying two pallet stones and the notch. Its angle from centre is `forkAngle`. *(spike)*
284	- **pallet stone** (pallet) — the jewel that stops and then pushes the escape wheel. **Entry** stone meets each tooth first; **exit** stone last. *(spike)*
285	- **locking face** — the stone face a tooth rests on while the wheel is stopped. *(spike)*
286	- **impulse face** — the inclined stone face a tooth slides along, pushing the fork. *(spike)*
287	- **locking corner** — where the two faces meet. **Let-off corner** — the far end of the impulse face, where the tooth leaves. *(spike)*
288	- **lock** — the phase with the tooth at rest on a locking face. `lockAngle` is the lever rotation needed to unlock. *(spike)*

## What it is (two sentences for a reviewer who has not seen it)

- A browser app where you learn why a mechanical watch runs fast or slow by fixing a simulated
  one. You read a timegrapher (rate in s/day, amplitude in degrees, beat error in ms), move the

## 2. What makes it interesting or non-obvious

Evidence in the repo:

- **It starts where the best existing explainer stops.** Ciechanowski's *Mechanical Watch* (2022)

- **The physics was checked, and the check changed the app.** Five research notes tag each claim
  as verified, plausible or unverified. Two findings overturned what had been assumed:
  - Claude told the research agent that a lever watch gains at low amplitude because of the
    escapement's impulse. Airy (1826), Hoyng (2015) and patent US 9,323,223 say the escapement
    alone makes it lose; the net gain in a flat-hairspring watch comes from the regulator pins.
    The model now has two separate terms and the puzzle text says so.
    (`docs/research/03-amplitude-and-isochronism.md` (b), (c); `docs/judgment-log.md` row 6)
  - A fall from 280° to 200° in a day was taken as a healthy watch. Sources put a healthy drop at
    10–15 %. So the healthy curve is 280° → 250°, and the slump puzzle uses a tired mainspring.
    (`docs/research/03-amplitude-and-isochronism.md` (a); `docs/judgment-log.md` row 7)
- **A sign that surprises.** A heavy spot at the bottom of the balance makes the watch lose above
  about 220° of amplitude and gain below it (the first zero of the Bessel function J1, 219.5°).

## 3. Key design decisions and tradeoffs

Each line: the decision, what it cost, where it is recorded.

- **Two layers.** A lumped oscillator model produces the numbers (period from inertia and
  hairspring stiffness, amplitude from mainspring torque against losses, beat error from stud
  offset, isochronism and position as explicit corrections). The escapement drawing is kinematic
  and follows the balance angle. Rate is never read off the drawing. Cost: the drawing cannot
  discover a rate effect on its own; every effect has to be put into the model deliberately.
  (`CLAUDE.md` "Two layers"; `src/core/oscillator/`, `src/core/escapement/`, `src/core/watch.ts`)
- **Simulation and rendering never mix.** The core is pure functions with tests; renderers read
  state and compute nothing. This is what let a separate design agent restyle the views without
  touching a test. (`CLAUDE.md` "Rules"; `tests/core/`, 49 tests in 7 files, plus the spike's 10)
- **Geometry is derived, never drawn.** Every shape comes from one parameter object. The same
  region is used for contact and for drawing. (`src/core/escapement/params.ts`, `shapes.ts`;
  `SPIKE.md` "What was built")
- **Escapement simplified, and labelled honestly.** Pointed teeth, zero draw, no guard pin, fork
  linear in balance angle. Research confirmed the angles but noted that pointed teeth are the
  English tooth form, so the UI says "lever escapement", not "Swiss lever". Draw's energy cost is
  a loss term in the model. (`CLAUDE.md` "Escapement fidelity";
  `docs/research/05-swiss-lever-escapement.md` "Spike parameters: verdict")
- **Two clocks.** The timegrapher accumulates ticks at 10× so a slope shows within seconds; the
  movement runs from real time down to 1/100 so one beat is visible. The screen says "60 s of
  ticks at 10× time". 60× was tried first and made the 60-second hold finish in one second.
  (`CLAUDE.md` "Two clocks"; `docs/judgment-log.md` row 11)
- **Completion is a held band, not a click.** A puzzle is solved when the instrument reading stays
  in the target band for 60 sim-seconds. The first build of puzzle 2 did not look at beat error at
  all and started filling the bar on load. (`src/puzzles/index.ts`; `docs/judgment-log.md` row 10)
- **Scope cuts.** One calibre, so the picker is hidden and the calibre is a static label. Lift
  angle is a fixed 52° label. Mobile means "does not break": panels stack, puzzle 1 is solvable.
  2D only. (`CLAUDE.md` "Picker", "First calibre", "Mobile means"; `watchmaker_brief.md` "Out")
- **Tests are restated in the open, never loosened quietly.** Three spike invariants were reworded
  because the original wording encoded a false symmetry. They are listed for the reviewer to
  judge. (`SPIKE.md` "Tests that were restated"; `docs/judgment-log.md` row 4)
- **Spike result: go, with one soft criterion failed.** Seven correction rounds against a target
  of three; two were wrong diagnoses. All three must-pass criteria passed, including Akash watching
  one beat at 1/20 speed. (`SPIKE.md` "Correction rounds", "Go / no-go scoring")
- **How the work was split.** Four agent lanes (physics / puzzles, design, onboarding, hosting)
  with file ownership and a one-line handoffs file for requests across lanes. `main` moves only on
  Akash's go-ahead in the deploying agent's own transcript. (`CLAUDE.md` "Ownership";
  `docs/handoffs.md`, 15 entries; `docs/agents/`; `.claude/skills/deploy/SKILL.md`)
- **Two project skills, each written for a failure that actually happened.** `verify-render`
  (screenshots plus numeric geometry checks) and `deploy` (verify the commit in a throwaway
  worktree, push, wait for Cloudflare, confirm the URL; no force pushes). (`.claude/skills/`)
- **Hosting.** Cloudflare Pages over Vercel: no commercial-use clause, no bandwidth cap, nothing
  needs serverless. Every branch gets a preview URL. (`docs/hosting.md`)

# The Watchmaker's Bench: design rationale

Akash Parikh · Live: https://watchmakers-bench.pages.dev · Repo: https://github.com/ahparikh/watchmakers-bench

The Watchmaker's Bench is a browser app where you learn why a mechanical watch runs fast or slow
by fixing a simulated one. A lever escapement and balance tick in slow motion in the centre of the
page. Beside them is a timegrapher, the instrument a watchmaker listens with, showing rate
(seconds per day), amplitude (degrees) and beat error (milliseconds). Each of four puzzles hands
you a faulty watch and a symptom. You move the adjustments a watchmaker has and read the result
on the instrument. There is no backend and no LLM at runtime.

## Why this theme and this approach

I like exploring a complex topic until it makes sense, and I love mechanical watches. To me they
are the perfect blend of art and science, with function and form in one object. I have long
admired Bartosz Ciechanowski's *Mechanical Watch*, which explains how every part works. I started
a Claude session to find an idea in that territory, and it fit Theme 1, Exploration &
Understanding.

We landed on two ideas. The first was a site that builds on Ciechanowski's article: now that you
know how a watch works, adjust one to repair it. The second was a tool for looking back over past
Claude sessions to improve future ones. The second was there only as a fallback, because I was
not sure the first would work.

So before spending real time I asked Claude to write a spike plan. The riskiest part was the
escapement: would it lock and release correctly, in sync with the balance, and look right? The
plan gave the spike 45 minutes and a written go / fallback / pivot rubric (`watchmaker_brief.md`).
The spike passed its three must-pass criteria, including me watching one beat at 1/20 speed. It
missed one soft criterion: seven correction rounds against a target of three (`SPIKE.md`). I chose
to go.

## What makes it interesting

A mechanical watch is very complex, but the watchmaker's skill is something else again: hearing
that a watch is wrong and knowing which part to touch. Most explanations stop at how it works.
This one lets you debug a watch. It follows Ciechanowski's article and does not compete with it;
the first-visit guide is built from a summary of his article that maps each part to a slider
(`docs/onboarding/ciechanowski-summary.md`).

Two things were less obvious than I expected:

- **One puzzle has no fix.** In "Afternoon slump" the watch gains by evening and no slider cures
  it. The regulator moves the whole day's rate up or down; it cannot flatten the curve. The puzzle
  ends with a question, and the answer is a new mainspring. Some errors are physics, not
  adjustment.
- **Checking the physics changed the app.** Every physics claim was researched against sources
  before it became code (`docs/research/`). Claude had asserted that the escapement makes a watch
  gain as its swing shrinks. Airy (1826), Hoyng (2015) and a patent say the escapement alone makes
  it lose; the gain comes from the regulator pins. The model was split into two terms and the
  puzzle text now says so. Separately, a heavy spot at the bottom of the balance makes the watch
  lose above about 220° of amplitude and gain below it. Claude wrote the opposite expectation; the
  model, built from the research note, was right.

## Key decisions and tradeoffs

- **2D, not 3D.** A flat technical drawing, with every shape derived from one set of named
  parameters. Ciechanowski already did 3D well. 2D was cheaper to get correct and easier to check
  with numbers. The cost: you never see the movement as an object you could hold.
- **Physics first, proven by a spike.** The numbers come from a small oscillator model with tests
  (49 in the core). The escapement drawing is a separate layer that follows the balance, and rate
  is never read off the drawing. Simulation and rendering never mix, which let a design agent
  restyle the page without touching a test. The cost: the drawing cannot discover an effect by
  itself; each one has to be put into the model on purpose.
- **One simple calibre.** A time-only, 18,000 bph movement. The catalog is built for more, but the
  picker stays hidden until a second one exists. The escapement is simplified (pointed teeth, no
  draw), so the app says "lever escapement", not "Swiss lever".
- **An onboarding flow that shows what each slider does.** A first visit opens an eight-step
  guide. Each step lights the part on the drawing that a slider moves, and each puzzle shows only
  the sliders it needs. This cost a good share of the iteration time and was worth it: without it
  a newcomer faces seven controls and three numbers with no way in.
- **Two clocks.** The timegrapher runs at 10× so a trend shows in seconds; the movement runs in
  slow motion so one beat is visible. The screen labels the time span honestly.
- **Parallel agents with clear lanes.** Once the spike passed I ran separate Claude agents for
  physics and puzzles, design, onboarding, and hosting. Each owned named files, and requests
  across lanes went into a one-line handoffs file (`docs/handoffs.md`). Two project skills cover
  the failures that kept happening: `verify-render` (screenshots plus numeric geometry checks)
  and `deploy` (verify, push, confirm the URL). Production moves only on my go-ahead.

The tools did not catch everything, and `docs/judgment-log.md` records each miss. Two were mine to
find. The impulse pin was drawn on the wrong side of the fork; every screenshot was frozen at the
one angle where right and wrong look the same, so the checks passed. I saw it watching one beat in
slow motion. Later the wind slider flickered at the point where the watch stops. Claude blamed
four different things in turn before my exact repro showed a readout changing width under the
pointer. Each fix added a test or a script so it stays fixed.

## With more time

- **More complex calibres.** This watch only keeps time. A date, a stopwatch (chronograph) or
  other complications would bring new mechanisms and new physics, and new faults to find.
- **3D.** Rotate the movement and view it from different angles.
- **Harder puzzles.** Faults with more than one cause, where the first fix exposes the second.
